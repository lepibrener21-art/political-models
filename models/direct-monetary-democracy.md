# Direct monetary democracy

**In one line:** no taxes; citizens keep their earnings and fund public projects directly, so money replaces votes.

## Mechanism
- Anyone may propose a concrete public project with a budget.
- Citizens give their own money to projects they back; no compulsory tax.
- Funded projects run; unfunded ones wait in a backlog or shut down.
- A project that stops delivering stops receiving money.

## Origins
- Voluntary taxation in Auberon Herbert's voluntaryism [herbert1885].
- Tools for funding public goods without compulsion: assurance contracts [tabarrok1998], quadratic funding [buterin2019].
- Milder relative: "tax choice" (earmarked taxation).

## Real-world use
- No state has funded itself this way.
- Partial versions: Hungary's 1% law (1996) and Italy's *5 per mille* (2006) let taxpayers direct a slice of income tax to NGOs; Seattle democracy vouchers (first used 2017); Gitcoin's quadratic funding of open-source software.
- Money-weighted voice has a poor record: Prussia's three-class franchise (1849–1918) weighted votes by taxes paid.

## Strengths
- Spending is a costly, honest signal of how much people care.
- Fast accountability: failing projects lose money now, not at the next election.
- No one is forced to fund what they object to.
- Pork cannot hide inside large bundled budgets.

## Weaknesses
- One dollar, one vote: the rich outweigh everyone else, and projects serving the poor go unfunded.
- Free riders: non-excludable goods (defense, clean air, research) are underfunded when giving is voluntary [samuelson1954] [olson1965]; in experiments, contributions decay without punishment [fehr2000].
- Courts, police and rights protection cannot depend on popularity; unpopular rulings would be defunded.
- Visible projects beat maintenance; volatile revenue rules out long-term commitments and borrowing.
- Interdependent projects (a hospital and its road) need coordination, i.e. planning.
- Too many projects to judge: delegation to advisors and marketers returns.
- Property rights and fraud enforcement are public goods that must be funded first.

## Open questions
- Would a hybrid work: a compulsory core budget (courts, audit, debt, safety net, maintenance) plus an equal civic budget per person to direct? It should score far higher on protection and popular power; compare `participatory-budgeting`.
- Can all-or-nothing thresholds, milestone payments and lifetime-cost rules curb overpromising?
- Small, voluntary, high-trust groups (co-ops, DAOs) may manage now; where is the size limit?
- Freedom is disputed: high on paper, but weak protection drags it down in practice.
