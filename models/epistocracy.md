# Epistocracy

**In one line:** political power is weighted by knowledge, e.g. extra votes or veto rights for citizens who pass a competence test.

## Mechanism (variants)
- Restricted suffrage: only those passing a test vote.
- Plural voting: extra votes for the educated (J.S. Mill).
- Epistocratic veto: a knowledgeable council can block laws.
- "Government by simulated oracle": correct voters' choices for what they would choose if well informed.

## Origins
- Plato's philosopher-kings; J.S. Mill's plural voting [mill1861].
- Modern defense: Jason Brennan, *Against Democracy* [brennan2016].

## Real-world use
- None as such. Literacy tests in history were used to exclude groups, especially in the US South; a key warning.

## Strengths
- Directly targets voter ignorance.
- Useful as a provocation: it forces democrats to justify equal votes.

## Weaknesses
- Whoever writes the test holds the real power; history shows tests become tools of exclusion.
- Knowledge correlates with wealth and education, so it can entrench privilege.
- Undermines legitimacy and equal dignity.

## Open questions
- Can deliberation (see `sortition`) deliver the benefit of informed decisions without unequal votes?
