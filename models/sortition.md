# Sortition and citizens' assemblies

**In one line:** a randomly selected, demographically representative group of citizens studies an issue, hears experts, deliberates, and recommends or decides.

## Mechanism
- Random selection, stratified by age, gender, region, education.
- Members are paid, briefed by experts on all sides, and deliberate in facilitated groups.
- Output is usually a recommendation to parliament or a question put to referendum.
- Variant: deliberative polling, measuring how opinions change after deliberation [fishkin2009].

## Origins
- Athens selected most officials by lot.
- Modern revival: Fishkin's deliberative polling (1988 onward) [fishkin2009]; Van Reybrouck's *Against Elections* [vanreybrouck2016].

## Real-world use
- Ireland: Constitutional Convention (2012–14) led to the 2015 same-sex marriage referendum; Citizens' Assembly (2016–18) led to the 2018 abortion referendum.
- Ostbelgien (German-speaking Belgium): permanent citizens' council since 2019.
- France: Citizens' Convention for Climate (2019–20); many proposals later diluted by government.

## Strengths
- Mirrors the population better than elected bodies; no campaign money or party machines.
- Deliberation tends to produce informed, less polarized conclusions.
- Hard to bribe or capture a body chosen by lot and rotated often.

## Weaknesses
- Weak legitimacy if politicians can ignore the outcome (France).
- Agenda-setting and expert selection by organizers shape results.
- Members are not accountable to voters; nobody chose them.
- Self-selection: invited people can decline, skewing the sample.

## Open questions
- Should a sortition chamber ever have binding power, e.g. as a second chamber?
- How to protect assemblies from organizers' framing?
